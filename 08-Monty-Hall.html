The Monty Hall paradox

⏱ 60 minutes🎯 Conditional probability🎮 Simulation🧠 Intuition and reasoning

A television game

Three doors, one car, and two goats. The game looks simple, but the best choice surprises almost everyone.

The rules
1. Choose
The contestant chooses one door. Behind one door is the car; behind each of the other two is a goat.
2. Monty opens
The host knows where the car is and always opens an unchosen door that hides a goat.
3. Decide
The contestant may stay with the initial door or switch to the only other door still closed.
The crucial rule: Monty does not open a door at random. He knows where the car is, never opens the contestant’s chosen door, and always reveals a goat.

First impression

After Monty opens one door, two closed doors remain. Do you think the probabilities are now 50% and 50%?

When Monty opens one of the two unchosen doors and reveals a goat, the total probability of 2/3 does not disappear or split in half: it remains associated with the one unchosen door that is still closed.
Conditional probability

Let:

  • C1: the car is behind the initially chosen door;
  • C2: the car is behind the other door that remains closed;
  • H: Monty opens a specific door and reveals a goat.

The vertical bar is read as “given that”. After the host’s action, we are interested in conditional probabilities:

P(C1 | H) = 1/3     e     P(C2 | H) = 2/3

The key point is that H is not randomly generated information: Monty knows where the car is and follows precise rules.

Explore: calculation with Bayes’ theorem

Suppose you chose door 1 and Monty opens door 3. We want to calculate the probability that the car is behind door 2.

P(C2 | H3) = [P(H3 | C2) · P(C2)] / P(H3)

If the car is behind door 2, Monty must open door 3, so P(H3 | C2) = 1. Also, P(C2) = 1/3.

The probability that Monty opens door 3 is:

P(H3) = (1/2 · 1/3) + (1 · 1/3) + (0 · 1/3) = 1/2

Therefore:

P(C2 | H3) = (1 · 1/3) / (1/2) = 2/3

Similarly, P(C1 | H3) = 1/3.

What if Monty opened a door at random?

The result would change. If the host could randomly open the door containing the car, the information “a goat was revealed” would arise from a different probability mechanism. In the classical problem, Monty’s strategy is an integral part of the experiment.

Rules matter

The advantage of switching depends on how the host generates the new information.

Why is the host so important?
He knows the prize location
Monty always knows which door hides the car.
He reveals a goat
He always opens an unchosen door containing a goat.
He always offers the switch
The opportunity to switch does not depend on what is behind the initially chosen door.
These assumptions are part of the probability model. If the host opened a door at random, could reveal the car, or offered the switch only in some situations, the probabilities would be different.
A thought experiment

To make the mechanism more visible, imagine — purely as a thought experiment — a game with many doors: one car and goats behind all the others. The first choice would have a very small probability of being correct; almost all the probability would lie among the unchosen doors.

Because the host knows the car’s location, he would eliminate only goat doors and leave one other door closed in addition to your chosen door. This is not meant as a realistic TV scenario; it simply makes clear that the information provided by Monty is not random.

The point is not the extreme number of doors. The point is that Monty chooses which doors to open using information that the contestant does not have.

A famous paradox

Monty Hall has become a symbol of the gap between intuition and probabilistic reasoning.

Monty Hall

The problem is named after Monty Hall, the long-time host of the US television show Let's Make a Deal. The mathematical version became especially famous after Marilyn vos Savant answered a published question in 1990: switching doors doubles the probability of winning.

The answer triggered intense criticism, including from readers with scientific training. The controversy shows just how counterintuitive the problem is.

Why does intuition go wrong?
Apparent equiprobability
We see two closed doors and automatically assign 50% to each.
Ignored information
We forget that Monty knows where the car is and does not open a door at random.
Attachment to the first choice
Switching may feel psychologically risky even when it is rationally advantageous.
