The mean stabilises

The Law of Large Numbers describes the convergence of the running mean, not the disappearance of randomness from individual outcomes.

From Bernoulli to the Law of Large Numbers
Jacob Bernoulli (1654–1705)

In theArs Conjectandi, published posthumously in 1713, proved that the relative frequency of an event tends to stabilise around its probability as the number of trials increases.

An idea that became general

Bernoulli’s result concerned repeated trials with two outcomes. In later centuries, the Law of Large Numbers was extended to much more general situations.

What do we observe?

After n observations, the running mean is:

x̄n = (x1 + ··· + xn) / n

If the experiment is repeated under the same conditions, as n increases, the mean tends to approach the expected value μ.

x̄n → μ
What does it not say?
  • The next outcome does not become predictable.
  • The sequence does not become regular.
  • Deviations do not have to cancel immediately.
  • Convergence does not mean exact equality after a predetermined number of trials.
Important: the overall mean stabilises. Individual outcomes remain random.
The false myth of compensation

A fair coin has landed heads ten times. On the next toss, has tails become more likely?

The Law of Large Numbers concerns the behaviour of a mean in the long run. It is not a force that immediately corrects an unbalanced sequence.
Further detail: a more precise formulation
In its weak form, for every ε > 0, the probability that the mean differs from μ by more than ε tends to zero as n increases:
P(|X̄n − μ| > ε) → 0
This formula is optional: the essential message remains the stabilisation of the running mean.

What is changing?

As the number of rolls in each group increases, what tends to take on a bell shape?

A normal shape emerges

The Central Limit Theorem explains why sums and means of many independent contributions often have an approximately normal pattern.

A history developed over time
de Moivre and Laplace

In the eighteenth century, Abraham de Moivre showed that the binomial distribution can be approximated by the normal curve. Pierre-Simon Laplace developed and generalised this idea.

Lyapunov and Lindeberg

Between the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, they formulated more general conditions under which sums of many contributions tend towards a normal distribution.

LLN

Law of Large Numbers

Follow one sequence and increase the number of observations.

x̄n → μ

The value of the running mean.

CLT

Central Limit Theorem

Repeat many groups of the same size.

shape of sums/means → normal

The shape of the distribution of results.

Sum and mean: optional formulas

Sum

Sn = X1 + ··· + Xn
E(Sn) = nμ
Var(Sn) = nσ²

Mean

X̄n = Sn/n
E(X̄n) = μ
Var(X̄n) = σ²/n
Means obtained from larger groups are more concentrated around μ: their standard deviation is σ/√n.
It does not mean that everything becomes normal

The theorem requires conditions. In its simplest version, the observations must be independent, generated by the same probability structure, and have finite mean and variance.

Already symmetric distribution

The normal shape may emerge with relatively small groups.

Skewed distribution

It may be necessary to increase the group size substantially.

Very heavy tails

Convergence may be slow, or the usual form of the theorem may not apply.

From bars to the curve

The binomial distribution is discrete. When the number of trials increases and conditions are favourable, a normal distribution with the same mean and variance can approximate its profile very well.

Example: chocolate promotion

A brand places a promotion in its chocolates: each draw has probability p = 0.20 of winning another chocolate. After each draw, the initial conditions are restored.

Replacement

The mechanism is returned to its initial state each time.

Independence

The result of one trial does not change the probability of subsequent trials.

Constant probability

The proportion of winning chocolates always remains 20%.

These conditions allow the total number of wins to be modelled with a binomial distribution.
X ~ Bin(1000, 0.20)

We want to calculate the probability of obtaining at most 190 successes:

P(X ≤ 190)

The exact binomial calculation requires summing 191 probabilities. With 1,000 trials, we can use a normal distribution with the same mean and variance:

μ = np = 1000·0.20 = 200
σ² = np(1−p) = 160
σ = √160 ≈ 12.65
Y ~ N(μ = 200, σ = 12.65)

1. Identify the threshold

The question asks for the probability of obtaining at most 190 successes. The threshold to transform to the standard-normal scale is therefore 190.

P(X ≤ 190) ≈ P(Y ≤ 190)

2. Standardise using μ and σ of the normal distribution

z = (190 − μ)/σ = (190 − 200)/12.65 ≈ −0.79

The value −0.79 indicates that 190 is approximately 0.79 standard deviations below the mean of the approximating normal distribution.

3. Find z in the table

P(Z ≤ −0.79) ≈ 0.2148
The approximate probability of obtaining at most 190 wins is therefore approximately 21.5%. The value is obtained from the standard normal distribution after transforming 190 into the corresponding z-score.
See the connection with normal tables
The procedure exactly follows the previous e-tivity: transform the original threshold into a z-score and then read the area to the left of that value in the cumulative table.
What if there were only 10 chocolates?

With n = 10 and p = 0.20, the expected number of wins is only 2. The binomial distribution is still highly discrete and skewed: the normal approximation is not sufficiently accurate.

Exact binomial

For example:

P(X ≤ 1) = P(X=0)+P(X=1) ≈ 0.3758
Normal approximation

Standardising the threshold 1:

z = (1−2)/√1.6 ≈ −0.79
P(Z ≤ −0.79) ≈ 0.2148
The results are very different. With few trials, the exact binomial distribution must be used. Increasing n does not automatically make the approximation good: both np and n(1−p) must be sufficiently large.

When is the approximation weaker?

Why does this matter in Politics, Law and Economics?

Many political, legal, and economic outcomes result from the sum of numerous small and uncertain contributions. Large numbers and the central limit explain why aggregates can be more stable than individual cases.

Electoral participation

A citizen votes or does not vote. Across a large random sample, the observed turnout proportion tends to stabilise around the population probability.

Law of Large Numbers: the running turnout proportion stabilises around the population turnout probability.
Aggregate demand

Household expenditure is highly variable. The mean expenditure across many sampled households tends to be more regular and may approach a normal shape.

Central Limit Theorem: aggregation generates a more predictable shape.
Institutional monitoring

The number of successful applications in a large set of independent cases can be modelled with a binomial distribution and, when appropriate, approximated by a normal distribution.

This makes some calculations involving thresholds and intervals quicker.
Public expenditure and service times

An individual expenditure or case-processing time may vary greatly. Means and sums across many cases tend to be more stable and useful for planning, budgeting, and service capacity.

More observations do not eliminate uncertainty, but they make aggregate indicators more regular.

Two short worked examples

Read the question, identify the required method, and then follow the calculation and interpretation.

Worked example 1

Standard error of the mean

Question. A population has mean 50 and standard deviation 20. For samples of size 100, find the mean and standard error of the sampling distribution of X̄.

Solution. E(X̄)=50 and SE(X̄)=20/√100=2. The sample mean remains centred on the population mean but varies less than individual observations.
Worked example 2

Law of Large Numbers or CLT?

Question. A researcher says that, as sample size grows, the sample average tends to stabilise near the population mean. Which result is being invoked?

Solution. The Law of Large Numbers. The Central Limit Theorem instead describes the approximate shape of the sampling distribution after suitable standardisation.

Large samples and the Central Limit Theorem
Individual household expenditure is strongly right-skewed. Explain why the mean expenditure from samples of 200 households may nevertheless have an approximately normal sampling distribution.
Show the guided solution

The Central Limit Theorem concerns the distribution of sample means, not the distribution of individual observations. With independent observations, finite variance, and a sufficiently large sample, the standardised sample mean is approximately normal even when individual expenditure is skewed.
