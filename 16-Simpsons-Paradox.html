If we select an ESG fund at random:

P(Low risk | ESG) = 412/600 = 68,7%

If we select a traditional fund at random:

P(Low risk | Tradizionale) = 291/600 = 48,5%

The two types therefore do not have the same risk composition.

Learn more: why are the totals 6.2% and 7.1%?

The aggregate return is a weighted mean. The weights are precisely the conditional probabilities obtained from the table.

E(R | ESG) = 0.687 × 4.1 + 0.313 × 10.8 ≈ 6.2%
E(R | Traditional) = 0.485 × 3.8 + 0.515 × 10.2 ≈ 7.1%

The within-group returns have not changed. Only the weight assigned to low and high risk has changed. This is enough to modify, and sometimes reverse, the overall comparison.

An apparent paradox

The aggregate result is not false: it answers a different question from the within-group comparisons.

Why is it called this?

In 1951, Edward H. Simpson published The Interpretation of Interaction in Contingency Tables, an article that became the modern reference for this type of reversal.

Similar phenomena had already been discussed by Karl Pearson and George Udny Yule. The name “Simpson's paradox” became established later as Simpson's work became widely known.

It is not a mathematical contradiction. Aggregate means use different weights and can therefore tell a different story.

Confounding variable: risk is associated with both fund type and return. Ignoring it means comparing groups with different compositions.
Do not automatically disaggregate everything

The control variable must be plausible and relevant to the phenomenon being studied.

Do not confuse association with causation

The reversal signals an interpretive problem, but by itself it does not establish which relationship is causal.

A procedure for a written-exam answer

When an aggregate table suggests a conclusion, pause before interpreting it and check whether the result is stable within subgroups.

1Describe the total

Calculate aggregate percentages, means, or rates without immediately turning them into a causal explanation.

2Look for relevant variables

Consider risk, sector, area, period, channel, segment, or complexity.

3Condition the comparison

Construct separate tables or compare sufficiently homogeneous groups.

4Compare the stories

Check whether the direction and strength of association change after disaggregation.

Useful questions
Does time matter?

An aggregate association may conceal very different economic phases or periods.

Does the case mix matter?

Citizens, jurisdictions, socioeconomic groups, or cases may have different initial levels of difficulty or risk.

Is the variable plausible?

Do not disaggregate at random: a theoretical or substantive reason is needed.

A rule of caution: do not choose the aggregate or disaggregated table merely because it confirms the preferred conclusion. The research question and a plausible mechanism should guide interpretation.

Find the error in the headline

The marginal figures are correct, but the headline turns an aggregate comparison into a general conclusion. Identify the hidden variable and then examine the conditional tables.

Two short worked examples

Read the question, identify the required method, and then follow the calculation and interpretation.

Worked example 1

A numerical reversal

Question. In easy cases, method A succeeds in 9/10 cases and B in 72/90. In hard cases, A succeeds in 18/90 and B in 1/10. Compare within groups and overall.

Solution. Within easy cases A is better (90% vs 80%); within hard cases A is also better (20% vs 10%). Overall, however, A is 27/100=27% and B is 73/100=73%. The unequal allocation of easy and hard cases reverses the aggregate comparison.
Worked example 2

How to answer in the exam

Question. An aggregate table and the corresponding subgroup tables point in opposite directions. What should the conclusion say?

Solution. State both results, identify the stratifying variable, explain that group composition is a confounder, and base the substantive interpretation on the comparison that is appropriate to the research question. Do not report only the aggregate percentage.
Now try one yourself · 4 points
Marginal and conditional comparisons
A policy appears more effective overall in Region A, but within both urban and rural areas it is more effective in Region B. Explain how this reversal can occur.
Show the guided solution

The regions may contain different proportions of urban and rural units, and baseline outcomes may differ strongly between those strata. The marginal comparison mixes composition and within-stratum effects. Compare conditional outcomes within relevant strata and report how group composition produces the reversal.
