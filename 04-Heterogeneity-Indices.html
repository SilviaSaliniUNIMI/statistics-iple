Heterogeneity Indices

Gini and Shannon: measuring how concentrated a categorical distribution is in a few categories or how diversified it is across many categories.

Variability exists even without numbers

For a qualitative variable, we cannot calculate distances between categories. We can, however, observe how units are distributed among them. If all units belong to the same category, the distribution is homogeneous; if they are evenly distributed, it is heterogeneous.

Section 2

Two ways of measuring diversity

The Gini and Shannon indices operate on relative frequencies \(f_i\). Both equal 0 when one category contains the entire distribution and reach their maximum when the categories are equally likely.

Gini heterogeneity index
\[H_G=1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_i^2\]

The sum of squares increases when a few categories dominate. Subtracting it from 1 gives a measure of differences among categories.

Minimum: 0. Maximum: \((k-1)/k\).

\[H_{G,norm}=\frac{H_G}{(k-1)/k}\]
Shannon index or entropy
\[H_S=-\sum_{i=1}^{k}f_i\log(f_i)\]

It measures the uncertainty associated with the category of a randomly selected unit. If all categories are equally likely, uncertainty is at its maximum.

Minimum: 0. Maximum: \(\log(k)\).

\[H_{S,norm}=\frac{-\sum f_i\log(f_i)}{\log(k)}\]
The logarithm base. The raw Shannon value changes when using the natural logarithm, base 2, or base 10. The normalised index does not change, provided the numerator and denominator use the same base.
Why normalise?

The theoretical maximum depends on the number of categories. With 2 categories, the raw Gini index can reach only 0.5; with 5 categories, it reaches 0.8. Normalisation places both indices on the 0–1 interval and facilitates comparison.

Section 3

Corrado Gini, Claude Shannon, and the history of the indices

The two indices originated in different scientific contexts: statistics of variability and mutability on the one hand, and the mathematical theory of communication on the other.

Corrado Gini (1884–1965)

An Italian statistician, demographer, and sociologist, born in Motta di Livenza, in the Treviso area. He studied variability, mutability, and concentration, making a decisive contribution to the development of summary indices for describing distributions.

Key works:

Variability and Mutability (1912)
On the Measurement of Concentration and Variability of Characteristics (1914)

Claude E. Shannon (1916–2001)

An American mathematician and engineer, regarded as the founder of information theory. In 1948 he published A Mathematical Theory of Communication, showing that information and uncertainty could be quantified.

Shannon entropy is now used far beyond telecommunications: in ecology, linguistics, computer science, machine learning, network analysis, and many other fields.

For more than ten years, Treviso has hosted a festival devoted to the public communication of statistics, demography, and data culture. The event is jointly organised by the Association Corrado Gini, by the Italian Statistical Society (SIS) and the Italian National Institute of Statistics (ISTAT). Its location in Treviso recalls the connection with the area where Corrado Gini was born.

Section 6

Where are heterogeneity indices used?

The same mathematical idea—describing how much variety and balance exist among categories—recurs in very different disciplines.

🌿

Politics

Party-system fragmentation, coalition diversity, and balance among parliamentary groups.

🛒

Law

Distribution of case types, judicial outcomes, and legal remedies across categories.

👥

Economics

Export diversification, sectoral composition, and concentration of economic activity.

🏭

Public administration

Distribution of expenditure, service requests, or policy instruments across categories.

🌳

Machine learning

Gini impurity and entropy are criteria used in classification trees to make nodes more “pure”.

💬

Linguistics and information

Lexical variety, distribution of symbols, predictability of sequences, and message compression.

An index describes; it does not judge. High heterogeneity in a party system does not automatically imply representation, stability, or democratic quality. Additional questions, indicators, and information are required.
A note on measures of concentration

Corrado Gini also developed measures of concentration for transferable quantities, such as income and wealth, which are now often associated with the Lorenz curve. They are not the same as the categorical index studied here and are mentioned only briefly.

Preparing for the exam

This final section contains two short worked examples followed by a 4-point practice question in the style of the IPLE written examination.

Two short worked examples

Read the question, identify the required method, and then follow the calculation and interpretation.

Worked example 1

Gini–Simpson heterogeneity

Question. Three political affiliations have relative frequencies 0.50, 0.30, and 0.20. Calculate 1 − Σp².

Solution. 1 − (0.50² + 0.30² + 0.20²) = 1 − 0.38 = 0.62. The index is the probability that two randomly selected individuals belong to different categories.
Worked example 2

Same mode, different diversity

Question. Distribution A is (0.60, 0.20, 0.20) and distribution B is (0.60, 0.39, 0.01). They have the same modal category. Are they equally heterogeneous?

Solution. No. A spreads the remaining 40% evenly, whereas B concentrates almost all of it in one category. A therefore has greater categorical heterogeneity even though the mode and its frequency are the same.
Now try one yourself · 4 points
Heterogeneity of a categorical distribution
Parliamentary seats are distributed as 40%, 30%, 20%, and 10% across four parties. Calculate the Gini heterogeneity index and interpret it.
Show the guided solution

H_G = 1 − (0.40²+0.30²+0.20²+0.10²) = 1 − 0.30 = 0.70. The distribution is fairly diversified, but it is not maximally heterogeneous: with four equally represented parties the maximum would be 0.75.
