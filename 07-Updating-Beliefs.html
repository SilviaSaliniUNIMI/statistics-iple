How valuable is a signal?

New information can greatly change what we think, but its meaning also depends on what we knew beforehand.

First, your judgement

A disease affects 5% of people. The test is positive for 80% of people with the disease and for 5% of healthy people. A person tests positive: what is the probability that they actually have the disease?

1. Before the test
The disease affects 5 people out of 100.
2. New information
The test is positive, but it can also be positive for a healthy person.
3. After the test
The probability changes by combining the initial frequency and the reliability of the test.
Common error: confusing the probability of testing positive given that one has the disease with the probability of having the disease after a positive result.
P(+ | disease) ≠ P(disease | +)
Why begin with a diagnostic example? Because it immediately reveals an error in intuitive reasoning. In the following sections, we will use the same principle in public health, legal screening, credit policy, and fraud detection.

Now restrict the group

We are no longer choosing a person from all 100. We know that the test is positive, so we consider only the 9 positive cases. Among these, 4 have the disease.

P(disease | positive) = 4 / 9 = 44,4%
This is a conditional probability. The vertical bar “|” is read given that: P(disease | positive) is the probability of having the disease given that the test is positive.
How do we describe test quality?
Sensitivity
We consider only people with the disease: how many correctly receive a positive result?
4 / 5 = 80%
Specificity
We consider only healthy people: how many correctly receive a negative result?
90 / 95 = 94,7% ≈ 95%
Sensitivity limits false negatives; specificity limits false positives. Neither is the same as P(disease | positive).
Prior probability
Before the test: P(disease) = 5%.
Posterior probability
After a positive test: P(disease | positive) = 44.4%.

From conditional probability to Bayes

New information narrows the reference space. Bayes allows the updated probability to be calculated without constructing the table every time.

PriorWhat we know beforehand
×
EvidenceHow compatible the signal is with the hypothesis
→
PosteriorWhat we believe afterwards
P(A | B) = P(B | A) · P(A) / P(B)
Reading the formula
P(A)
Prior: initial plausibility of A.
P(B | A)
How likely B is if A is true.
P(B)
How likely B is in general.
P(A | B)
Posterior: updated plausibility of A.
P(M | +) = [P(+ | M)P(M)] / [P(+ | M)P(M) + P(+ | not M)P(not M)]
P(M | +) = (0,80 × 0,05) / [(0,80 × 0,05) + (0,05 × 0,95)] = 0,444 = 44,4%
Why? The numerator represents people who have the disease and test positive. The denominator represents all people who test positive: true positives plus false positives. This is the same 4/9 ratio observed in the table.
How is the formula derived?
The joint probability can be written in two ways: P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B)P(B) and P(A ∩ B) = P(B | A)P(A). Equating the two expressions and dividing by P(B) gives Bayes’ theorem.
Further detail: total probability
If A and not A exhaust all possibilities, then P(B) = P(B | A)P(A) + P(B | not A)P(not A). This is the denominator of the diagnostic formula.

Evidence for policy and legal decision-making

An algorithm, a sensor, or market research does not provide certainty: it produces evidence that updates probabilities.

Probability and decision are not the same thing

A fraud alert raises the probability of fraud from 0.5% to 20%. This is a strong update, but it does not mean that the transaction should automatically be blocked.

Cost of a false negative
The fraud goes undetected.
Cost of a false positive
A legitimate application is blocked.

Bayes updates what is plausible. A policy or legal choice also requires costs, benefits, rights, and consequences.

Mental shortcuts

Heuristics help us make decisions quickly when time and information are limited. They are often useful, but they can produce biases: systematic errors in probabilistic judgement.

Representativeness
An applicant “looks like” the typical profile of a high-risk case and is judged risky, even when the default rate in the population is very low.
Confirmation bias
Someone convinced that a policy will succeed tends to seek favourable signals and minimise contrary ones. Bayesian reasoning instead requires the assessment to be updated even when the evidence points in the opposite direction.
Availability heuristic
After hearing news about fraud or a corporate failure, the event seems more frequent because it is easy to recall, not because the data actually show an increase.
Base-rate neglect
A highly convincing alert captures our attention, and we forget how rare the phenomenon was before the alert. This is the central error in the diagnostic example and in many fraud-detection systems.
The contribution of Bayes
It does not eliminate heuristics, but forces us to make the starting point, quality of evidence, and updating process explicit. In this way, intuition can be compared with reasoning that can be checked.

A different way of doing statistics

Bayes’ theorem is not merely a formula for reversing conditional probabilities. It is the principle underlying an entire approach to inference: start from what we know, observe the data, and update uncertainty.

From a formula to a statistical method
PriorInformation before the data
×
LikelihoodHow compatible the data are with possible values
→
PosteriorUpdated uncertainty
Posterior distribution ∝ Likelihood × Prior distribution

In Bayesian statistics, even an unknown parameter is described through a probability distribution. Before observing the data, we have a prior distribution; after observing the data, we obtain a posterior distribution.

Key idea. The parameter does not change when the data arrive: our uncertainty about its value changes.
Frequentist approach
The parameter is fixed but unknown. Probability describes the behaviour of procedures in hypothetical repetitions of sampling.
Bayesian approach
Probability also expresses uncertainty about the parameter. Initial information and data are combined in the posterior distribution.
This is not a competition between two schools. They are two different ways of formalising uncertainty. The choice depends on the problem, the available information, and the objective of the analysis.

Where is it used today?
Forecasting
Update forecasts of economic demand, electoral support, or risk.
A/B testing
Progressively evaluate policy messages or public-information variants.
Political communication
Estimate participation, support, and response to public messages.
Credit and fraud
Update risk after each new piece of evidence.
Institutional risk
Combine historical data, expert judgement, and new evidence.
Decisions and forecasts
Combine initial information and new data to update estimates and choices.
Well-known applications. Bayesian reasoning has been used in searches for missing objects, assessment of rare risks, classification, medicine, predictive maintenance, and decision models.
Why is it so important today?

For many years, Bayesian methods were used less than their theoretical strength would suggest, mainly because they required very complex calculations. The development of computers, statistical software, and new numerical algorithms has profoundly changed the situation.

Today, Bayesian statistics is one of the main approaches to statistical inference. It is used in scientific research, economics, finance, political science, medicine, data science, and numerous artificial-intelligence applications.

Before
Many models could only be applied to relatively simple problems.
Today
It is possible to update estimates and forecasts in complex models, including when data arrive progressively.
Why it is useful. Bayesian inference makes it possible to integrate prior information and new data, continually update estimates, and represent explicitly the uncertainty accompanying a decision.
Remember. Bayes’ theorem is a rule of probability. Bayesian inference uses that principle to learn from data and draw conclusions about parameters, hypotheses, and predictions.

Preparing for the exam

This final section contains two short worked examples followed by a 4-point practice question in the style of the IPLE written examination.

Two short worked examples

Read the question, identify the required method, and then follow the calculation and interpretation.

Worked example 1

Bayes’ rule with a rare event

Question. A condition affects 2% of a population. A test has sensitivity 90% and a 5% false-positive rate. Find P(condition | positive).

Solution. P = (0.90×0.02)/[(0.90×0.02)+(0.05×0.98)] = 0.018/0.067 ≈ 0.269. A positive result raises the probability substantially, but it is still only about 27% because the prior prevalence is low.
Worked example 2

Why the base rate matters

Question. The same test is used in a high-risk group with prior probability 20%. Without calculating, should the posterior probability after a positive result be higher or lower?

Solution. Higher. The likelihood information is unchanged, but the prior odds are larger. Bayes’ rule combines both pieces of information, so the same positive result has a different meaning in populations with different base rates.
