Three tools for counting

The formulas are not the starting point. They are shortcuts that avoid listing every outcome one by one.

Permutations

We use all the elements, and order matters.

Example: in how many orders can 6 people present?

Pn = n!

6 people

The first position can be filled in 6 ways, the second in 5, then 4, 3, 2, and 1.

6! = 6×5×4×3×2×1 = 720
Further detail: repeated elements
If some elements are indistinguishable, permutations that differ only by swapping identical elements are not new. For example, with repetitions of sizes n₁,…,nₕ:
n! / (n₁! · n₂! · … · nₕ!)
Arrangements

We use only k elements out of n and order matters.

Example: assigning three distinct roles to 10 candidates.

Dn,k = n! / (n−k)!

10 candidates, 3 roles

10 choices for the first role, 9 for the second, and 8 for the third.

10×9×8 = 720
Further detail: repetition allowed
If each position can be filled by choosing again from the n elements, there are n choices for each of the k positions:
D′n,k = nk
Combinations

Usiamo k elements out of n, but order does not matter.

Example: choosing a committee of 3 people from 10.

Cn,k = (n su k) = n! / [k!(n−k)!]

Why divide by k!?

Each group of k people has been counted k! times, once for each possible internal order.

D10,3 / 3! = 720 / 6 = 120

From counting to probability

When all elementary outcomes are equally likely, counting transforms a space of possibilities into a probability.

Election monitoring

A batch contains 12 items, 3 of which are flagged. If 4 are inspected without replacement, what is the probability of finding at least one flagged item?

Possible samples
C12,4

We choose 4 items out of 12.

Favourable samples
C12,4 − C9,4

All samples minus those containing no flagged items.

Probability
1 − C9,4 / C12,4

We use the complement: at least one = 1 − none.

What does this have to do with statistics?

Statistics uses data collected from part of reality to learn about a broader set. Probability helps us understand which samples we could have obtained and how plausible each result is.

Further detail: why can different samples tell different stories?
When we choose only some units from a population, the composition of the sample also depends on chance. Another sample, drawn using the same method, might contain different units and produce a different result. Probability allows us to describe this uncertainty. Later in the course, we will use this idea to move from observed data to more general conclusions.

Two short worked examples

Read the question, identify the required method, and then follow the calculation and interpretation.

Worked example 1

Distinct public offices

Question. Eight candidates are available for three distinct posts: chair, deputy chair, and secretary. How many assignments are possible?

Solution. Order matters because the posts are different: 8×7×6 = 336. This is an arrangement without repetition, not a combination.
Worked example 2

Selecting a committee

Question. A ten-member council must select a three-person committee with no distinct roles. How many committees are possible?

Solution. Order does not matter: C(10,3) = 10!/(3!7!) = 120. The same three people form one committee regardless of the order in which they are listed.
