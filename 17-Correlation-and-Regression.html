Learn more: the covariance formula
Cov(X,Y) = (1/n) Σ (xᵢ − μX)(yᵢ − μY)
sXY = [1/(n−1)] Σ (xᵢ − x̄)(yᵢ − ȳ)

The first formula describes population covariance; the second is sample covariance. Covariance is positive when contributions from quadrants I and III predominate and negative when contributions from quadrants II and IV predominate. Its value, however, depends on the measurement units of X and Y.

Standardising covariance: Pearson correlation

Dividing covariance by the product of the standard deviations gives a unit-free index that always lies between −1 and +1.

Population
ρ = Cov(X,Y)/(σXσY)

ρ is the parameter describing linear correlation in the population.

Sample
r = sXY/(sXsY)

r is the correlation observed in the sample and is used to estimate ρ.

r = Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ) / √[Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² · Σ(yᵢ−ȳ)²]

Why does n−1 not appear in the final formula? If sample covariance and sample standard deviations are used, the factor 1/(n−1) appears both in the numerator and in the denominator and cancels. The same happens if 1/n is used consistently in all three terms. Therefore, r does not change when n or n−1 is used consistently.
However, ρ and r are not the same object. The formulas have the same structure, but ρ is a population parameter whereas r is a statistic calculated from a sample. In general, r is not exactly equal to ρ: it estimates ρ and changes from sample to sample.
Sign

A positive r indicates an increasing linear relationship; a negative r indicates a decreasing linear relationship.

Strength

An |r| close to 1 indicates a strong linear relationship; an |r| close to 0 indicates a weak linear relationship.

Shape

r measures only linearity: a curved relationship may have r close to zero even when the relationship is strong.

Bridge to regression: in simple linear regression with an intercept, R² = r². The sign of the relationship can be read from r or the slope; squaring r removes the sign.

The least-squares method

The residual measures the vertical distance between the observed and predicted values:

eᵢ = yᵢ − ŷᵢ

The fitted line chooses β̂₀ and β̂₁ by minimising the sum of squared errors (or residuals), denoted by SSE:

SSE = Σ[yᵢ − (β₀ + β₁xᵢ)]² = Σeᵢ²

Interpretation

β̂₁ is the expected mean change in Y when X increases by one unit. β̂₀ is the predicted value when X=0 and should be interpreted only when X=0 is plausible in the observed context.

Optional mathematical extension: deriving the OLS estimates

The least-squares line chooses the intercept and slope to minimise the function:

SSE(b₀,b₁) = Σ[yᵢ − (b₀ + b₁xᵢ)]²

Here b₀ e b₁ are candidate values. To find the minimum, calculate the partial derivatives and set them equal to zero:

∂SSE/∂b₀ = −2Σ(yᵢ−b₀−b₁xᵢ) = 0
∂SSE/∂b₁ = −2Σxᵢ(yᵢ−b₀−b₁xᵢ) = 0

This gives the normal equations:

nb₀ + b₁Σxᵢ = Σyᵢ
b₀Σxᵢ + b₁Σxᵢ² = Σxᵢyᵢ

From the first equation we obtain b₀ = ȳ − b₁x̄. Substituting into the second and simplifying:

β̂₁ = Σ(xᵢ−x̄)(yᵢ−ȳ) / Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²
β̂₀ = ȳ − β̂₁x̄
Why is it really a minimum? The matrix of second derivatives is:
H = 2 [[n, Σxᵢ], [Σxᵢ, Σxᵢ²]]
det(H) = 4nΣ(xᵢ−x̄)²
If X has variability, Σ(xᵢ−x̄)² > 0: H is positive definite, the SSE function is strictly convex and the solution is the unique global minimum.

Connection with mathematics: the procedure uses a function of two variables, partial derivatives and a system of two equations. The same idea extends to multiple regression through matrix algebra.

SST – Total Sum of Squares: Σ(yᵢ − ȳ)², the total observed variability in Y.
SSR – Regression Sum of Squares: Σ(ŷᵢ − ȳ)², the part of the variability explained by the line.
SSE – Error Sum of Squares: Σ(yᵢ − ŷᵢ)² = Σeᵢ², the part left in the residuals.
Why English abbreviations? SST, SSR and SSE are derived from Sum of Squares are common in textbooks and statistical output. Many programs simply show a column labelled Sum Sq, distinguishing the model and residual rows. Caution: notation is not completely uniform; in some sources SSR denotes sum of squared residuals, which is what we call SSE here. Always check the definition attached to the abbreviation.
SST = SSR + SSE     R² = SSR/SST = 1 − SSE/SST

In OLS regression with an intercept, total variability is decomposed into the part explained by the model and the residual part. R² compares the variability explained by the regression with the total variability in Y.

Assumptions first, then inference and prediction

Standard errors, t-tests, p-values, confidence intervals, and predictions can be interpreted only after the model and the conditions that make its use valid have been stated explicitly.

The simple linear regression model
Yᵢ = β₀ + β₁Xᵢ + εᵢ

The term εᵢ collects all factors not included in the line that cause an observation to deviate from the conditional mean of Y.

Correct linear form

The conditional mean is E(Y|X)=β₀+β₁X. If the relationship is curved, the model must be modified.

Independent observations

The sample is random and errors for different units are not systematically dependent on one another.

Variability in X

The X values are not all equal: without variability in the regressor, β₁ cannot be estimated.

Zero conditional mean

E(ε|X)=0: omitted factors must not vary systematically with X. This is the key condition for interpreting β̂₁ as an unbiased estimate.

Homoscedasticity

Var(ε|X)=σ²: error dispersion is constant. This assumption supports classical standard errors.

Conditional normality of the errors

ε|X ~ N(0,σ²). In small samples, this makes the usual t-tests, F-tests, and confidence intervals exact.

Diagnostics: linearity, constant variance, and the absence of excessively influential observations must be checked using the data and residuals. The next section shows why a single point can change the line.

Learn more: small samples, normality, and large samples

Small samples. If the errors are conditionally normal, ε|X ~ N(0,σ²), the least-squares estimators have a normal distribution conditional on the X values. Replacing σ with its estimate, the coefficient statistics follow an exact t-distribution with n−2 degrees of freedom. Under this framework, the usual tests and intervals are exact.

Maximum likelihood. Under normality and homoscedasticity, the least-squares estimates of β₀ and β₁ coincide with the maximum-likelihood estimates.

Large samples. Even without exact normality of the errors, under suitable regularity conditions and with finite moments, OLS estimators are asymptotically normal. t-based inference then becomes an approximation. In the presence of heteroscedasticity, robust standard errors must be used rather than the classical standard errors shown in the table.

Clarification. Marginal normality of X is not assumed. For exact small-sample inference, the requirement concerns the distribution of Y conditional on X, that is, normality of the errors.

From an explanatory model to a predictive model

Regression is not used only to interpret a relationship. It can also be used to predict a new value of Y and to quantify prediction uncertainty through the SER.

From an explanatory model to a predictive model
Interpretive use

The model helps us understand the relationship: the sign and magnitude of β̂₁ describe how Y changes on average as X changes. Tests and confidence intervals quantify uncertainty about the coefficients.

Predictive use

The model can also be used to estimate a future outcome. For a new value x₀, we substitute x₀ into the fitted line:

ŷ₀ = β̂₀ + β̂₁x₀
SER = √[SSE/(n−2)] = √(1,041.9/28) = 6.10 percentage points
SER

The Standard Error of the Regression, or residual standard error, measures the typical vertical distance between observed values and values fitted by the line. It is expressed in the same units as Y.

It is not SE(β̂₁)

SE(β̂₁)=0.31 measures uncertainty about the slope. SER=6.10 instead measures the dispersion of outcomes around the line and is therefore directly related to prediction precision.

R² and SER

R² is a relative, unit-free measure; SER is an absolute measure. Two models may have similar R² values but very different prediction errors in the operational units of the problem.

Learn more: interval for the mean and prediction interval

For a value x₀, the confidence interval for the mean response uses:

ŷ₀ ± t × SER × √[1/n + (x₀−x̄)²/Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²]

For a single future observation, the variability of the new error is added:

ŷ₀ ± t × SER × √[1 + 1/n + (x₀−x̄)²/Σ(xᵢ−x̄)²]

The additional term 1 makes the individual prediction interval always wider.

Bridge to subsequent predictive models: SER describes error within the sample used to estimate the line. When the main objective becomes predicting new cases, models must also be compared on data not used for estimation, using a test sample or cross-validation. It is not enough to select the model with more significant coefficients or the highest R².

After the output: diagnostics and influential points

The assumptions were stated before inference. Now we check whether the data and residuals show departures that may make the line or its standard errors unreliable.

Residual pattern

A curved pattern in the residuals indicates that the linear relationship is inadequate.

Residual dispersion

A funnel shape suggests non-constant variance and calls for revising the model or using robust standard errors.

Unusual observations

Extreme values in X or Y must be examined: they are especially influential when they combine high leverage with a large residual.

Dummy variables, interactions, and a complete written-exam interpretation

A dummy variable allows the regression model to compare groups. An interaction allows the relationship between X and Y to differ across groups. Always state the reference category before interpreting any coefficient.

Comparing two groups with an indicator variable

Let D=0 identify the reference group and D=1 identify the group we want to compare with it. The indicator variable allows the regression to compare the two groups while keeping the other explanatory variables fixed.

For example, if D=1 for union members and D=0 for non-union workers, the coefficient of D measures the expected wage difference between union and non-union workers with the same level of education.

Why is one group called the reference group? Its regression equation is the starting point. The coefficients attached to the indicator variable describe how the comparison group differs from that starting point.
1. A dummy changes the intercept
Y = β₀ + β₁X + β₂D + ε
Reference group: D=0
Ŷ = β₀ + β₁X
Comparison group: D=1
Ŷ = (β₀+β₂) + β₁X

The two groups have the same slope. The coefficient β₂ is the vertical difference between the two parallel regression lines at every value of X.

Example: union membership and wages.

wage = 4.20 + 0.75·education + 1.10·union

With union=0 as the reference category, union members are predicted to earn $1.10 more per hour than non-union workers with the same years of education. The education slope remains $0.75 in both groups.

2. An interaction changes the slope
Y = β₀ + β₁X + β₂D + β₃(X·D) + ε
Reference group: D=0
Ŷ = β₀ + β₁X
Comparison group: D=1
Ŷ = (β₀+β₂) + (β₁+β₃)X

Now β₂ changes the intercept and β₃ changes the slope. The slope for D=1 is β₁+β₃, not β₃ alone.

Example: political interest and turnout under compulsory voting.

turnout = 38 + 3.2·interest + 18·compulsory − 1.4·(interest·compulsory)

In voluntary-voting countries, a one-point increase in political interest is associated with 3.2 percentage points higher turnout. In compulsory-voting countries the slope is 3.2−1.4=1.8 points. Compulsory voting raises the predicted intercept by 18 points, but the association between interest and turnout is weaker.

More than two groups: do not turn categories into the numbers 1, 2, and 3

Suppose occupational position has three categories: administrative staff, senior officials, and executives. These labels describe groups; they are not measurements with known numerical distances.

A tempting but restrictive choice: entering the categories as Position=1, 2, and 3 treats occupational position as a quantitative variable. The model then forces the expected salaries to lie on a straight line and to be equally spaced.

What the values 1, 2, 3 would impose

A fitted line can only produce equally spaced predictions. With these three means, the fitted values would be approximately:

€27,500 · €49,000 · €70,500

The middle category is poorly represented because the model is trying to force all three means onto one straight line.

Why the values 1, 2, and 3 can distort the group means

The green horizontal segments represent the three observed group means. The orange line is the best fit obtained when occupational position is entered as the numerical values 1, 2, and 3. A single slope forces equally spaced predictions: it overestimates the mean for senior officials and underestimates the mean for executives (and also the first group).

Visual interpretation. With two indicator variables, the fitted function is a set of group-specific levels: each category receives its own intercept. Treating the categories as 1, 2, and 3 replaces these levels with one straight line and therefore imposes a pattern that the observed means do not follow.

Use two indicator variables instead

Take administrative staff as the reference group and create one indicator for senior officials and one for executives:

What happens to R²? On the same data, the model with separate indicator variables fits at least as well as the model based on the values 1, 2, and 3. Therefore, R² cannot be lower and may be substantially higher when the group means are not approximately aligned. The main reason for using indicators, however, is not merely to increase R²: it is to represent the categorical variable correctly.
Exam wording: “The occupational variable is categorical. Assigning the values 1, 2, and 3 would impose equal increments between adjacent categories. Using two indicator variables allows the three group means to differ freely, with administrative staff as the reference category.”
Exam-style output: a dummy that changes the intercept

Suppose the estimated wage model includes education and union membership:

wage = −1.84 + 0.694·education + 1.093·union

The standard error of the union coefficient is 0.201.

Interpretation

At the same education level, union members are predicted to earn about $1.09 more per hour than non-union workers, the reference group.

Significance

t = 1.093/0.201 ≈ 5.44. The coefficient is statistically significant at conventional levels.

Do not write: “union membership causes wages to rise by $1.09”. The regression supports a conditional association unless the study design justifies a causal claim.

Five short worked examples

Each example focuses on a different skill that may be required in the written examination.

Worked example 1

Look at the shape before using r

Question. A scatter plot is clearly U-shaped, but the correlation is r=0.04. Is there no relationship?

Solution. No. The correlation measures linear association. A value close to zero may coexist with a strong nonlinear relationship. The scatter plot must therefore be examined before interpreting r.
Worked example 2

Interpret and use the fitted line

Question. turnout = 38 + 2.1·education. Interpret the slope and predict turnout when education=10.

Solution. A one-unit increase in education is associated with an expected increase of 2.1 percentage points in turnout. For education=10, the predicted turnout is 38+2.1·10=59%. The intercept is the prediction when education=0 and should be interpreted only if that value is meaningful and within the observed range.
Worked example 3

From r to R², F, t and SE(β̂₁)

Question. With n=80, r=0.45 and β̂₁=1.20, recover the main regression statistics.

Solution. R²=r²=0.2025. Then F=[R²/(1−R²)](n−2)=19.81. In simple regression, t=+√F=4.45 because the slope is positive. Therefore SE(β̂₁)=1.20/4.45=0.27. The slope is statistically significant at conventional levels.
Worked example 4

R² and SER answer different questions

Question. A model has R²=0.18 and SER=7.4. What do the two numbers say?

Solution. R² says that 18% of the sample variation in Y is explained by the fitted linear model. SER measures the typical residual size in the units of Y: observed values are roughly 7.4 units away from the fitted line. A low R² does not by itself imply that the slope is not statistically significant.
Worked example 5

Indicator variable in a regression

Question. wage = −1.84 + 0.694·education + 1.093·union, where union=1 for union members and 0 otherwise. Interpret 1.093.

Solution. Non-union workers are the reference group. At the same education level, union members have a predicted hourly wage about $1.09 higher. Since there is no interaction, the two groups have parallel fitted lines and the education slope is the same in both groups.
Now try one yourself · 4 points
An integrated correlation and regression question
For n=100 countries, the correlation between education and voter turnout is r=0.50. The estimated model is turnout = 41 + 1.8·education and SER=8.0. A second model adds compulsory, an indicator equal to 1 for compulsory-voting systems and 0 otherwise. Its estimated coefficient is 6.0 with SE=2.0, and no interaction is included. Calculate and interpret R²; interpret the slope and predict turnout for education=10; test the slope using the relation between R², F and t and recover SE(β̂₁); finally, interpret and test the compulsory coefficient.
Show the guided solution

1. Correlation and goodness of fit. The association is moderately positive. R²=r²=0.50²=0.25, so the linear model explains 25% of the sample variation in turnout.

2. Slope and prediction. A one-unit increase in education is associated with an expected increase of 1.8 percentage points in turnout. At education=10, the predicted turnout is 41+1.8·10=59%.

3. Significance of the slope. F=[0.25/(1−0.25)](100−2)=32.67. In simple regression, t=+√32.67=5.72. Hence SE(β̂₁)=1.8/5.72≈0.315. The slope is statistically significant at conventional levels.

4. Indicator variable. Voluntary-voting systems are the reference group. Holding education constant, compulsory-voting systems are predicted to have turnout 6 percentage points higher. The test statistic is t=6/2=3.00, so the coefficient is statistically significant at the 5% level. Because no interaction is present, the education slope remains 1.8 in both groups.

Caution. These coefficients describe conditional associations unless the research design supports a causal interpretation.
