Misleading Graphs?

How to recognise, correct, and prevent misleading statistical displays

“A good graph is not the most spectacular one, but the one that reveals the structure of the data without distorting it.”

A graph is not decoration

A table and its corresponding graph should contain the same information. However, ordering, scale, bar shape, class width, and the choice between absolute values and ratios can radically change what the reader perceives.

Same data, two stories

Annual public expenditure rises from 9.8 to 10.5 million euros. With an axis from 0 to 11 million, the growth appears moderate; with an axis from 9.7 to 10.6 million, it appears spectacular.

Critical question

Is truncating the axis always wrong?

Practical rule. In bar charts, a zero baseline is normally necessary. In line charts, a restricted axis may be acceptable if it is clearly indicated and consistent with the analytical purpose.

Measurements on a continuous scale.

Nominal qualitative
Numbers that look like categories

Questionnaire software may treat the values 1, 5, 10, 50, 250 as equally spaced labels, even when the numerical distances are very different. This is acceptable only if the numbers are codes or class labels. If they represent quantities, the axis must preserve the distances.

Treated as categories

The five labels occupy equal spaces: the graph hides the fact that 250 is much farther from 50 than 5 is from 1.

Displayed on a numerical scale

The sticks are positioned at the actual numerical values. Distances on the horizontal axis preserve their quantitative meaning.

Example. “Population size” may be a quantity (1, 5, 10, 50, 250) or an ordered class (“1–9”, “10–49”, “50–249”, “250+”). The display depends on the meaning, not the appearance of the data.
Stick chart for a discrete quantitative variable

For a count, such as the number of monthly court filings, each value must occupy its own position on the numerical axis. The vertical segments remain separate: they are not adjacent rectangles as in a histogram.

Do not confuse the two. If 0, 1, 2, 5, and 10 are quantities, the distances must be respected. If instead they are category codes, the axis may be categorical, but this must be clearly stated.

Public-communication case · When a pie chart is appropriate and when it misleads

A pie chart represents parts of a single whole. Before using it, three conditions must be checked: the categories must be mutually exclusive, the shares must refer to the same denominator, and they must sum to 100%. Even when these conditions are met, too many slices make comparison difficult.

1. Appropriate use

Mutually exclusive categoriesTotal = 100%

Main information channel. Each citizen indicates only one channel: the four shares are non-overlapping parts of the same group.

2. Incorrect use: multiple responses

Overlapping categoriesOriginal sum = 165%

Channels used. Each citizen may select more than one channel. The software normalises 165% to 100% and creates a whole that does not exist in the data.

3. Visually correct, but difficult to read

Total = 100%9 similar categories

The categories are correct, but differences between similar slices are difficult to compare. An ordered bar chart communicates more clearly.

4. Correct representation for multiple responses

Percentage by channelThe total may exceed 100%

The bars retain the correct denominator: the percentage of residents who use each channel. They do not pretend that the categories form a single whole.

Diagnostic rule. Ask yourself: “Does each unit belong to only one slice?” and “Do the slices truly reconstruct a single whole?”. If either answer is no, do not use a pie chart. If there are many or very similar categories, prefer ordered bars.

The same data, different histograms

The number and width of the bins affect the perceived shape. With unequal intervals, the correct height is density, because the area of each bar must represent frequency.

A. Choose the number of bins
There is no single “true” number of bins. Too few bins may erase structure; too many may make noise look like a pattern. Rules such as Sturges, Scott, or Freedman–Diaconis are starting points, not substitutes for judgement.

The line chart preserves temporal order. The scale is stated explicitly and the variation is not concealed.

Two short worked examples

Worked example 1

A truncated vertical axis

Question. Support for a policy rises from 48% to 51%, but a bar chart starts at 47%. What is misleading?

Solution. The truncated axis visually magnifies a three-percentage-point increase. The values are correct, but the scale exaggerates the substantive change. The answer should report both the numerical difference and the graphical distortion.
Worked example 2

Choosing the correct graph

Question. You must compare voter turnout across six regions. Which display is preferable: a pie chart or a bar chart?

Solution. A bar chart is preferable because the task is to compare category values. A common baseline makes small regional differences easier to read than angles or sectors in a pie chart.

Reading a graph critically
A chart compares unemployment in two countries using bars that begin at 7% rather than 0%. Country A is at 8% and Country B at 9%. Explain why the graph may mislead and propose a better display.
Show the guided solution

The one-percentage-point difference is real, but the truncated baseline visually exaggerates it. A bar chart should normally begin at zero; alternatively, a clearly labelled dot plot can show the values and their difference without encoding them as full bar lengths.
